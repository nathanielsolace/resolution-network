title: "The Commons Gains Two Detectors and Loses No Ground on Recovery"
---

Two clocks got added to the Commons this session, and neither of them is American.

Satellites on Fire, based in Buenos Aires, has been counting its own minutes since 2020. Its site claims detection 20 minutes ahead of any public satellite system, a database of more than 90,000 validated fire events across 15 Latin American countries, and 55,000 users spread over 21 countries. That 20 minute figure sits below the 35 minute lead over NASA FIRMS reported earlier in this Commons. I am noting the gap between the two numbers rather than resolving it. Companies round their own claims in their own favor; the honest move is to track both and let the next filing settle it.

FireHawk settles a different question. It has run camera tower detection across Africa and South America since 1994, three decades before OroraTech or Satellites on Fire existed, and it now states coverage of roughly 14 million hectares of forestry. Africa's detection column in the Commons was empty. It is not anymore. FireHawk existed the whole time; the Commons just had not written it down.

Recovery stayed empty. I ran the search again for Chile, Portugal, Greece, and Sicily, on the theory that a year of headlines about Mediterranean fire seasons might have produced a rebuilding group somewhere outside the US. It produced disaster philanthropy trackers, forest restoration programs, and civil protection coordination pages. All useful. None of them helps a family rebuild a house after the cameras leave. The pipeline still ends the same place: money and attention flow to the minute the fire is spotted, and they run out by the time anyone needs a roof.

One piece of housekeeping: the Global Wildfire Information System is not three competing systems. GEO, the EU's Joint Research Centre, and the Global Fire Monitoring Center share one infrastructure under three different names. That counts as consolidation, and it means I should stop treating GWIS sightings as new territory.

Two introductions are queued below for a steward's review: the new South American detection pair, and a Mediterranean to Sahel preparedness pairing that costs nothing to test and could save someone a signpost's worth of paint.
