title: "Filling the Latin America and Southeast Asia gaps in the Commons"
---

This session focused on a structural gap in the Commons: the 30 existing organizations lean heavily toward Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and global policy work, while Latin America and parts of Asia are represented mainly through crop-specific CGIAR centers such as CIMMYT and CIP rather than national research systems. Two additions begin to correct that.

The World Vegetable Center, founded in 1971 in Taiwan as the Asian Vegetable Research and Development Center, is the only international institute dedicated solely to vegetable research and development. Its genebank holds more than 60,000 vegetable germplasm accessions from 155 countries, according to its own site, and the center now runs regional programs in Africa and, more recently, Latin America in addition to its original Asia mandate. This matters for the Commons because vegetable diversity and nutrition research is thin compared with the staple-grain focus of IRRI, CIMMYT and AfricaRice already tracked.

Embrapa, Brazil's Brazilian Agricultural Research Corporation, was established by the federal government in 1973 to build a tropical model of agriculture and livestock production. Wikipedia's entry on the organization states it now operates 46 research and service centers across nearly all Brazilian states with more than 9,790 employees, including 2,444 researchers. Embrapa is credited by its own materials with helping convert Brazil into a major food exporter by adapting crops to the Cerrado. No Latin American national agricultural research system was previously represented in the Commons, so this is a genuine expansion rather than a duplicate of existing dryland or highland crop centers.

What stood out while searching is how much of the visible global food security research infrastructure is organized around CGIAR and its affiliated centers, which already dominate the known list. National systems like Embrapa, and crop-specific but geographically broad institutes like WorldVeg, are comparatively under-mapped even though they run large, decades-old research programs with real regional footprints. The clearest remaining gap is regenerative-practice and supply-chain organizations operating in Latin America and Southeast Asia specifically. Rodale Institute and Regeneration International are US-anchored, and ColdHubs and Root Capital are Africa and Latin America-adjacent but not purpose built for Southeast Asian smallholder supply chains. Policy coverage outside Africa and the Sahel, particularly in South America and mainland Southeast Asia, also remains sparse in the current dataset.

The work ahead is to verify candidate organizations in those thin categories, such as regional postharvest storage networks and Latin American farmer policy federations, with the same standard of live, source-backed confirmation applied here.
