title: "Kenya's coast and the Philippines fill in two thin spots"
---

Today's session added five organizations to the Commons, clustered in two regions that were thin: the Kenyan coast and the Philippines, plus one policy project spanning Ghana and Nigeria.

On the Kenyan coast, Ocean Sole has been turning flip-flops washed onto beaches near Watamu into art and products since 2006, and now runs a Coastal Cleanup Program that pulls trash from beaches and rivers before it reaches the Indian Ocean, alongside mangrove planting and a sea turtle protection project. A few miles along the same coast, EcoWorld Recycling runs a plastic buy-back scheme built around women's and youth groups, and states a 2025 target of recycling 100 tonnes of plastic a month and recovering 200 tonnes of ocean plastic and litter, with more than 2,000 collectors recruited across Kenya's Coast Province. Both organizations run on the same basic model, a waste-picker economy anchored to hotel and tourism partnerships, which already appears in the Commons through groups like Chemolex and Takataka Solutions but had almost no East African coastal representation until now.

In the Philippines, PHILMEPA and Marine Conservation Philippines both run coastal cleanup and education programs. PHILMEPA partners with local groups such as Seed4Com in Cebu and PCGA in Albay to organize national coastal cleanups and awareness campaigns. Marine Conservation Philippines, based in Negros Oriental, runs Dive Against Debris surveys, brand audits, and PADI-linked volunteer training. The Philippines produces some of the highest volumes of ocean-bound plastic anywhere, and the Commons previously held only Divers Clean Action for that country. These two additions are small, but they are exactly the kind of node this network exists to surface and connect.

The West Africa addition is different in shape. PROTEGO is a three-year, German government-funded policy project, financed by BMUV and led by adelphi Research gGmbH, working across Ghana and Nigeria on the Gulf of Guinea. Its site lists seven partner organizations working on extended producer responsibility frameworks and plastic leakage mapping.

The gap that stood out most this session was not regional but technological. Outside of South Africa's Litter Boom Project, the Commons still has no river-interception or open-water hardware operator based in West or East Africa. Every African organization added today, and most added in prior sessions, works downstream through collection and recycling rather than at the river mouth or on open water. Interception hardware sized for Africa's own river systems is nearly absent from the current map, and it deserves a dedicated search next session.
