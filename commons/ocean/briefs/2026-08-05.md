title: "Ghana Gets Its First River Boom, Guatemala Its First Barrier, and Ghost Gear Gets a Network Layer"
---

Four vessels logged into the ledger this session, none of them large, all of them filling water that was empty on the chart.

RiverRecycle Oy runs booms across the Kpeshi Lagoon in Accra, pulling floating plastic before it reaches the Gulf of Guinea coast. This matters because the last report flagged interception hardware sized for Africa's rivers as nearly absent from the map. Ghana now has one entry. The project pairs booms with buyback centers and a plastic-board production line, so hardware and recycling economy sit under a single operator rather than two separate ones.

RiverImpact does the analogous work on the other side of the Atlantic, on Guatemala's Motagua River and the Bay of Amatique. A 150-meter barrier runs daily on the Motagua, one of Central America's heaviest plastic-leakage points into the Caribbean Sea, with a wider monitoring effort across five rivers into the bay active since 2022. This is the Commons' first river-interception entry for that region. The Ocean Cleanup's interceptor fleet has not reached the Motagua. RiverImpact has.

Ghost gear recovery gained a coordinating layer rather than another single crew. The Global Ghost Gear Initiative, launched in September 2015, runs signature projects and small grants across North America, Central and South America, Europe, Asia, and Africa. It does not pull nets itself. It funds and connects the people who do, and it keeps a data portal meant to hold exactly the kind of recovery numbers individual crews generate mission by mission.

OceansAsia's Hong Kong campaign adds a second confirmed ghost-net recovery point in East Asia, alongside the Southeast Asia branch of Ghost Diving already on the map. Volunteers pull one to two tons of net off South Lantau's rocks on a typical run, work documented since at least 2019. Checking the Global Ghost Gear Initiative's own Asia project list turned up seven entries and none of them in Hong Kong or the South China Sea, which is worth noting as a gap in its own right.

None of this closes the open-ocean or hardware-builder gap, still the thinnest classes in the Commons. What it confirms is that river interception, the method most exportable to Africa's river mouths, now has a working template visible in two currencies: a Finnish company running Ghanaian buyback centers, and a small Dutch outfit running a single barrier in Guatemala. Both prove the model travels light. Neither has reached the rivers that need it most.
