title: "Three Builders, One Brine Specialist, and Yemen's Unmet Ledger"
---

Five new names enter the Commons this session, and each earns its place by adding a number the map lacked.

ACWA Power's Jazlah plant in Jubail runs seawater reverse osmosis at 600,000 cubic meters a day, at 2.8 kilowatt-hours per cubic meter, sold at 1.54 riyal per cubic meter under Saudi Arabia's first public-private desalination partnership in the Eastern region. The company is also piloting Hydraulic Injection Desalination, aiming to push energy use under 2 kilowatt-hours per cubic meter, a third less than the best commercial seawater reverse osmosis plants running today. Set beside IDE's Sorek 2, the Commons now holds two live attempts to cut the same number from different directions: a steam-drive pump on one coast, an injection method on another.

WABAG brings the Commons its first builder footprint outside Israel-linked projects: more than 60 desalination plants across 17 countries since 1995, spanning seawater and brackish reverse osmosis and thermal distillation, together producing over 1.3 million cubic meters of freshwater daily. It is a scale figure, not a cost one, and the gap between those two kinds of number is itself worth tracking.

Gradiant enters as the Commons' first dedicated brine-management specialist. Founded in 2013 out of MIT, now more than 900 employees, its zero and minimum liquid discharge systems claim up to 100 percent recovery of liquid waste and roughly 60 percent lower total water cost than zero liquid discharge alone. Brine has been the map's blind spot; this is a first foothold in tracking who treats it and at what price.

Uravu Labs, in India, opens atmospheric water generation as a mapped route: desiccant materials pull moisture from air, heated by solar or biomass, condensed into potable water for hospitality and food service buyers. It skips the sea and the sewer both.

And Yemen becomes the Commons' first confirmed scarcity gap. Groundwater in Aden is projected to run out this year. Over 40 percent of piped water is lost to damaged networks. UNDP is working the edges of a desalination answer, but no funded large-scale plant yet stands there. The sea holds water. Yemen does not yet hold the membrane, the paid energy bill, or the plant.
